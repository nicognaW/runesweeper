## Complexity: what is known and what it implies for exact solvers

### Takeaway
Deciding whether a board is consistent is NP-complete (Kaye 2000). Deciding whether a given cell is forced, the "inference" question a solver asks, is co-NP-complete (Scott, Stege, van Rooij 2011). Counting consistent mine layouts, which is what exact probabilities need, is #P-complete. Playing to maximize the win probability is PP-hard, and in one formulation PSPACE-complete (de Bondt 2012). In practice, exact solvers enumerate per frontier component, and this works on standard boards. Fully optimal play (a game-tree search) is affordable only in small endgames.

### Cited Findings
- Kaye, "Minesweeper is NP-complete", *The Mathematical Intelligencer* 22(2), 9–15 (2000). It defines **Minesweeper Consistency**: is a partially revealed board consistent with some mine placement? It proves the problem NP-complete by reduction from Boolean circuit SAT. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Minesweeper-is-NP-complete-Kaye/16005748b9607cdf4bb87e504019a526f617820d); [ACM DL "Minesweeper as an NP-complete problem"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1113847.1113873)
- Scott, Stege, van Rooij, "Minesweeper May Not Be NP-Complete but Is Hard Nonetheless", *The Mathematical Intelligencer* 33(4), 5–17 (2011). It defines **Minesweeper Inference**: given a board promised to be consistent, is some cell forced (safe or mine)? It shows this problem is co-NP-complete. This refines Kaye's result rather than overturning it. Consistency is not the problem a player faces, because a real board is always consistent. — (snippet) [search summary citing the paper; see also Utrecht thesis](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/20.500.12932/48804/Scriptie%20(5).pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)
- Becerra's 2015 Harvard thesis also explains "why the decision problem is co-NP-complete". — [UiTM catalogue record of Harvard DASH thesis](https://openaccess.library.uitm.edu.my/Record/ndltd-harvard.edu-oai-dash.harvard.edu-1-14398552)
- **Counting** the solutions of a Minesweeper configuration is #P-complete. Pedersen's 2004 thesis "The complexity of Minesweeper and strategies for game playing" proves it. — (snippet) [Pedersen 2004 PDF](https://minesweepergame.com/math/the-complexity-of-minesweeper-and-strategies-for-game-playing-2004.pdf). A Minesweeper history page attributes the #P-completeness proposal to Nakov & Wei, who model the game as an MDP. The primary Nakov & Wei paper was not found. — (snippet) [minesweepergame.com math papers list](https://minesweepergame.com/math-papers.php)
- Golan, "Minesweeper on graphs" (2011), restates that consistency is NP-complete and counting is #P-complete. It gives **polynomial-time algorithms on trees and on graphs of bounded treewidth**. — (snippet) [Golan 2011 PDF](https://minesweepergame.com/math/minesweeper-on-graphs-2011.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220557823_Minesweeper_on_graphs)
- de Bondt, "The computational complexity of Minesweeper" (arXiv 1204.4659, 2012) studies the game itself, not single-position inference. Locating all mines with the highest probability is **PP-hard**. When the probability of locating all mines may be infinitesimal, the game is **PSPACE-complete**. — (snippet) [arXiv 1204.4659](https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.4659); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-computational-complexity-of-Minesweeper-Bondt/fec46b2cc99ecb91723cbac63a01c0e110b7c49f)
- A separate result shows a move-limited Minesweeper variant is PSPACE-complete, "even when p is a constant, in particular p = 1". It appears to be the AAAI paper "Minesweeper with Limited Moves". — (snippet, attribution uncertain) [aitopics "Minesweeper with Limited Moves"](https://aitopics.org/doc/conferences:0F39ED0A)
- Recent theory includes the following. "Complexity of Planar Graph Orientation Consistency, Promise-Inference, and Uniqueness, with Applications to Minesweeper Variants" (arXiv 2404.14519, 2024) covers consistency, promise-inference and uniqueness for variants. "Phase transition for Minesweeper" (arXiv 2506.01634, 2025) studies how solvability depends on mine density. "The Minesweeper Game: Percolation and Complexity" (Combinatorics, Probability and Computing, 2002) is older. — [arXiv 2404.14519](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.14519); [arXiv 2506.01634](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.01634); [CUP record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/combinatorics-probability-and-computing/article/minesweeper-game-percolation-and-complexity/C96A8C5AF6E7FC75C389483020B4DCA3)
- In practice an exact probability engine is fine on standard boards. David Hill's JSMinesweeper says its exact engine's cost "grows exponentially", but "50x50/500 should be fine, 100x100/2500 might struggle". — [DavidNHill/JSMinesweeper README](https://github.com/DavidNHill/JSMinesweeper)
- Practical caps on game-tree search: JSMinesweeper switches to brute-force endgame analysis only when "the number of solutions remaining is 750 or less". Hill's Java solver runs "brute force deep analysis", a game tree that maximizes win probability, when there are fewer than **4,000** candidate solutions. One logged run had 1,008 solutions, 67,984 nodes, 28,432 cache entries and 11,453 cache hits, and took **253 ms**. — [JSMinesweeper README](https://github.com/DavidNHill/JSMinesweeper); [DavidNHill/Minesweeper README](https://github.com/DavidNHill/Minesweeper)

### Inferences
- The worst-case results are about adversarial or constructed boards. On random boards at standard densities (about 12–21% mines), the frontier splits into small, weakly coupled components. Studholme's and Becerra's "coupled subsets" and "connected components" methods exploit this. Exact enumeration per component, combined with the global mine count by combinatorial weighting, stays tractable. Hill's "50x50/500 fine" note supports this.
- The real hardness for "optimal" play is the game tree: PP-hard or PSPACE-complete. This is why every strong solver plays greedily or with one-step look-ahead, and searches the full tree only below a few thousand remaining layouts.
- Golan's bounded-treewidth result suggests a principled exact method for large frontiers: dynamic programming over a tree decomposition of the frontier. It avoids the full product of component enumerations. No published Minesweeper solver benchmarking this approach was found.

### Gaps
- Kaye's later result that infinite Minesweeper is Turing-complete was not confirmed from a reachable source in this session.
- The exact formal statement and venue of de Bondt's PSPACE-completeness result (what "infinitesimal" means formally) could not be checked, because arXiv was blocked.
- The primary Nakov & Wei paper was not located, and nor was its MDP formulation or any results.
- Authorship and year of "Minesweeper with Limited Moves" were not confirmed.

## Published solver approaches and their measured win rates

### Takeaway
Published solvers progressed in this order: single-point rules, then CSP enumeration (Studholme 2000), then probability-guided CSP with heuristics (Pedersen 2004, Becerra 2015), then UCT/POMDP hybrids (Buffet et al. 2012), then tuned heuristic combinations (Tu et al. 2017, "PSEQ"), then exact probability plus look-ahead and endgame tree search (Hill's solvers, roughly 2019 onward). Hill's solvers report the best classic-rule Expert rate, about 40–41%. Learning-based agents (DQN, CNN, bandits) remain well below CSP and probability solvers on Expert, at about 10–24% versus 33–41%.

### Inferences
- **Superseded results**:
  - Studholme's 33.9% Expert (2000) was beaten by Buffet et al. (38.7%, 2012), TeddySweeper (38.84%, 2013), Tu et al. PSEQ (39.616%) and quasi-optimal (≥40.06%, 2017), then Hill (about 40–41%, classic safe start).
  - Becerra's 32.90% Expert (2015) was already below the 2012–2013 state of the art when published. It is often re-cited as "the CSP baseline", for example in arXiv 2212.10446.
  - The win rates in Pedersen's 2004 thesis (25% Expert) are also superseded.
- Learning-based agents have not approached exact-probability solvers on Expert: about 10% (CNN) and about 24% (DDQN, hobby project), against about 40%. No reviewed RL or NN paper found in this search claims parity on Expert.
- The gains from 2000 to 2017+ come mainly from (a) exact probabilities that use the global mine count, (b) where-to-guess heuristics (corners, information gain, progress), (c) look-ahead, and (d) endgame tree search. They do not come from better deterministic inference, which was essentially complete by 2000.

### Gaps
- Exact tables in Becerra 2015, Legendre et al. 2012 and Buffet et al. 2012 were not readable (hosts blocked). Missing details include per-algorithm rates for single-point and connected-components variants, games per run, and the first-click rule.
- No figures were found for Nakov & Wei.
- No 2023–2026 arXiv RL or transformer paper with a strong Expert result was found. The coverage of recent ML work is therefore incomplete.

## Best known win rates per level and rule, and the theoretical optimum

### Takeaway
Best reported Expert (30×16, 99 mines) rates are about **40–41% with a safe (not necessarily zero) first click**, from Tu quasi-optimal (≥40.06%) and Hill (40–41%). With a **zero/opening first click** the best is about **53–54.3%** (Hill). Intermediate's best is about 78% (safe first click). Beginner's best depends on the board: about 81.6–81.8% on 8×8 versus about 91–92% on 9×9. No proven theoretical optimum exists for any standard level. Tu et al.'s "quasi-optimal" bound and Hill's endgame-optimal play are the closest estimates.

### Cited Findings
- Expert, safe first click: Tu et al. PSEQ **39.616(5)%**, quasi-optimal **≥40.06(2)%** — (snippet) [HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/245453). Hill **41%** (corner start) and **40%** ("safe but not always zero") — [JSMinesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/JSMinesweeper); [DavidNHill/Minesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/Minesweeper)
- Expert, zero/open first click: Hill **54.3%** (open start at (3,3)) and **53%** (zero start) — [JSMinesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/JSMinesweeper); [DavidNHill/Minesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/Minesweeper)
- Intermediate (16×16, 40 mines): Tu PSEQ **78.122(8)%**, quasi-optimal **≥78.22(3)%**; TeddySweeper **77.84%**; mrgris with corners **77.4%** — (snippet) [HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/245453); [minesweepergame.com list](https://minesweepergame.com/math-papers.php); [mrgris](https://mrgris.com/projects/minesweepr/)
- Beginner: 8×8/10 (Windows 98 preset) at TeddySweeper **81.52%**, Tu **81.627%** and quasi-optimal **≥81.79%**. 9×9/10 at TeddySweeper **91.78%**, mrgris with corners **91.4%**, Becerra **91.25%**. — (snippet) [minesweepergame.com list](https://minesweepergame.com/math-papers.php); [HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/245453); [mrgris](https://mrgris.com/projects/minesweepr/). Lordeiro et al. state the presets as "beginner (8×8, 10 mines), intermediate (16×16, 40), expert (16×30, 99)" from Windows 98. — (snippet) [arXiv 2007.12824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12824)
- Unreviewed benchmark dataset (Hugging Face "LKForge/minesweeper-solver-benchmark"), 1,000 games per level: **95.5%** Beginner 9×9, **85.6%** Intermediate, **36.2%** Expert. It states best-known Expert solvers fall at about 34–40%. **78.5%** of Beginner games and **3.8%** of Expert games need no guess. — (snippet) [Hugging Face dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/LKForge/minesweeper-solver-benchmark)
- A popular guide puts the "theoretical max" Expert rate at about 30–40%. This is not rigorous, and Hill's 41% exceeds it. — (snippet) search summary of popular strategy pages, e.g. [minesweeperblast.com](https://minesweeperblast.com/how-to-win-minesweeper/)

### Inferences
- Tu et al.'s "Beginner" of about 81.6% matches TeddySweeper's 8×8 figure (81.52%), not its 9×9 figure (91.78%). So Tu's Beginner is almost certainly **8×8/10**, the Windows 98 preset. Studholme's 80.0% and Buffet's 80.2% are probably also 8×8. Becerra's 91.25% and mrgris's figures are 9×9. **Comparing Beginner numbers across papers without checking 8×8 against 9×9 is a common error.** 8×8/10 has a higher mine density (15.6% against 12.3%), which explains the roughly 10-point gap.
- The Hugging Face numbers of 95.5% and 85.6% exceed every published safe-first-click result. They most likely use a zero/opening first click. With only 1,000 games, the 95% interval is about ±1.3 points at 95% and about ±3.0 points at 36%. Treat this source as weak.
- Optimum estimates: classic Expert optimal play is probably only slightly above 41%. Tu's quasi-optimal (≥40.06%) and Hill's tree-searching solver (41%) converge from different methods, and look-ahead or tree search adds only about 0.5–1.5 points over a good greedy policy. This is an inference, not a proven bound.
- The zero-start rule adds about 13 points on Expert (41% to 54%). The first-click rule matters more than any solver improvement since 2000.

### Gaps
- No rigorous upper bound or exact optimal win probability for any standard level was found. Exact optimum computation is known only for tiny boards (UCT is described as "state of the art on small boards"), with no figures retrieved.
- Hill's game counts and confidence intervals are not stated on the fetched README pages.
- The best Intermediate and Beginner rates under a zero-start rule were not found from a reliable source.

## Benchmarking methodology

### Takeaway
No benchmarking standard is shared across papers. The results that matter depend on: board preset (8×8 versus 9×9 Beginner), first-click rule (safe cell, safe zero/opening, or unprotected), opening cell (corner, (3,3), centre), and game count (anything from 1,000 to millions). Tu et al. are the only source here that reports standard errors explicitly, as 81.627(8)%. Exact enumeration is normally capped by solution count rather than by wall-clock time.

### Cited Findings
- **First-click rules** in use:
  - "Classic or traditional": the first click is safe but not always a zero. The Windows behaviour relocates the mine.
  - "Modern": the first click is always a zero/opening.
  - Some ML papers allow the first click to be a mine; one such paper reports 52.4% on Beginner.
  - Hill reports both classic and modern rates. — [DavidNHill/Minesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/Minesweeper); [JSMinesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/JSMinesweeper); (snippet) [arXiv 2212.10446](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.10446)
  - Under traditional rules the first probe is always safe. — (snippet) [Tu et al. 2017](https://minesweepergame.com/math/exploring-efficient-strategies-for-minesweeper-2017.pdf)
- **Start position**: Studholme, Becerra and Tu et al. all favour corners. Studholme and Becerra argue that corners are most likely to be zeros, and Tu et al. find corner probing raises success. Hill benchmarks the classic rule with a corner start and the modern rule with an open start at (3,3). mrgris shows a corner preference adds about 4.7–8.2 points (Expert 33.1% to 37.8%, Intermediate 69.2% to 77.4%, Beginner 84.8% to 91.4%). — (snippet) [Tu et al.](https://minesweepergame.com/math/exploring-efficient-strategies-for-minesweeper-2017.pdf); [JSMinesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/JSMinesweeper); (snippet) [mrgris](https://mrgris.com/projects/minesweepr/)
- **Number of games and precision**:
  - mrgris: 100,000 games.
  - Hugging Face dataset: 1,000 per level.
  - DDQN project: 25,000.
  - Tu et al.: uncertainty in parenthesized last-digit notation, ±0.005 to ±0.008 points for PSEQ. If those are binomial standard errors, they imply on the order of 10^7 games. That is an inference, since the game count was not retrieved. — (snippet) [mrgris](https://mrgris.com/projects/minesweepr/); [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/LKForge/minesweeper-solver-benchmark); [Minesweeper-DDQN](https://github.com/AlexMGitHub/Minesweeper-DDQN); [HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/245453)
- **Other metrics reported**: Hill reports guesses per win (3.3 on classic Expert). The Hugging Face dataset reports the share of no-guess games (78.5% Beginner, 3.8% Expert). — [JSMinesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/JSMinesweeper); (snippet) [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/LKForge/minesweeper-solver-benchmark)
- **Paired comparison tooling**: Hill's repo has `MinesweeperBulk` and `MinesweeperBulkCompare` modules for bulk and head-to-head runs. Their method (shared seeds or not) is undocumented on the README. — [DavidNHill/Minesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/Minesweeper)
- **Runtime limits and hard instances**:
  - Hill caps exact work by solution count: 750 for brute force in JSMinesweeper, under 4,000 for deep analysis in the Java solver.
  - The probability engine is exponential, "fine" at 50×50/500 and possibly struggling at 100×100/2500.
  - Teytaud's slides cite 10,000 UCT simulations per move as Buffet et al.'s budget (garbled source). — [JSMinesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/JSMinesweeper); [DavidNHill/Minesweeper](https://github.com/DavidNHill/Minesweeper); [Teytaud slides](https://slideshare.net/teytaud/mines-ics2)

### Inferences
- Recommended protocol, synthesized from the above:
  - Fix the board preset explicitly (W×H/M).
  - Fix the first-click rule (safe-cell versus zero) and the opening cell.
  - Generate boards from recorded seeds *after* the first click, applying the rule.
  - Run every solver on the same seeds, so differences are paired and the variance is lower.
  - Report n, the win rate and a binomial 95% interval (Wilson). Also report guesses per win, the no-guess fraction and time per game or move.
  - Cap exact enumeration by solution count or frontier size, with a deterministic fallback such as sampling or a heuristic, so results stay reproducible.
- Resolving a 0.5-point difference near 40% at about 2σ needs about 4×0.24/0.005² ≈ 38,000 games per solver if unpaired. Paired seeds reduce this a lot. So 1,000-game benchmarks cannot separate modern solvers.
- Hard instances for exact enumeration are boards with a long, connected frontier and many undetermined cells, typical of mid-game Expert or high-density custom boards. Expert's 20.6% density sits near where the 2025 "phase transition" paper locates the solvability change. The link is plausible but was not verified.

### Gaps
- None of the retrieved sources documents seed handling, paired comparisons or timing methodology in detail.
- How Tu et al. obtained their uncertainty figures, and how many games they ran, could not be confirmed.
- No published benchmark of hard frontier instances for exact enumeration (runtime against frontier size) was found.
